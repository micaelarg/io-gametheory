Bidding for Argentina's Medicines

A walkthrough of an analysis run with an open-source game-theory skill, step by step: the questions we typed, the data it went after, the games it wrote down, the code it ran and the objections that changed its mind. The answer: no cartel, but drug tenders with almost no competition, and a Ministry of Health that has mostly stopped tendering.

How to read this page

The skill applies the same seven steps to every question. Each section below is one step, in the order they ran. Commands are the skill's own scripts; outputs are real and trimmed for length. Questions were asked in Spanish; translations are below each one.

STEP 1

Frame the question

"Corré la skill sobre las licitaciones de laboratorios en Argentina.""Run the skill on drug-laboratory tenders in Argentina."

The skill turned that into a decision question before touching any data:

  • Who decides: the Ministry of Health and other federal buyers; laboratories and distributors that bid.
  • Positive question: are drug prices set by competition, and if not, by what?
  • Compared to what: the same items with more bidders, and the same drugs bought under a credible alternative (importing, pooled purchasing).

STEP 3

Write the game down

The data showed two different ways the State buys medicines, so the skill wrote two games.

Game A · tenders

A procurement auction

Players: the bidders on each drug item. Action: a sealed price. Information: each knows its own cost, not the others'. Payoff: price minus cost if it wins, zero otherwise. Prediction: bidders shade their price above cost, and the shading shrinks as more bidders show up.

Game B · direct awards

Bargaining with a monopolist

Players: the Ministry and the patent holder. Action: offers and counteroffers. At stake: what the treatment is worth to the State minus what it costs to make. Prediction: the price lands between the two, capped by the State's best credible alternative.

STEP 4

Solve it with code

Game A: how much competition is there?

First the simplest count: how many bids each item received.

Then the auction model: from observed bids, the skill recovers each bidder's implied cost and markup (Guerre-Perrigne-Vuong). If competition works, markups fall as bidders are added.

The same pattern shows up without any model, in the court-ordered purchases: the cushion between the winner and the runner-up.

Game A: is anyone colluding?

Bid-rigging screens look for groups of firms that always bid together and take turns winning, cover bids clustered above the winner, and firms that bid but never win.

Game B: how often does the Ministry skip the auction?

From purchase orders, the share of Ministry of Health drug spending awarded without a tender, measured within each year so inflation does not distort it.

$ uv run merger.py hhi --shares minsalud_tendered_2023_25.csv - Firms: 41; CR4 = 76.8%; effective number of equal-sized firms 1/HHI = 2.9 - HHI = 3,403 (highly concentrated; no merger specified) $ uv run merger.py hhi --shares minsalud_direct_2023_25.csv - Firms: 37; CR4 = 46.9% - HHI = 820 (unconcentrated) # sellers are many patent holders, each a monopolist on its own drug
STEP 5

Find what would flip the answer

  • The third bidder. The cushion and the markup fall sharply between two and four bidders. Recruiting one more firm beats any reserve-price tweak (Bulow-Klemperer), and a third of all items (most of the Ministry of Health's) never reach two bids.
  • A credible outside option. In direct deals the price is capped by the State's best alternative. If importing costs about 15% over the international reference price, any price above roughly 1.15× means that alternative was not on the table. An earlier comparison of 16 purchases found 1.13× for pembrolizumab and 2.1× for nivolumab bought through a distributor.
  • Could the tendered market sustain a cartel? With about two effective firms and monthly purchases, yes, even if cheating were noticed a year late:
$ uv run collusion.py sustain --model bertrand --n 2 --lag 12 - Critical discount factor δ* = 0.500 (closed form 0.500 = 1 − 1/n) | detection lag | δ* | longest gap between interactions that sustains collusion | | 1 | 0.500 | 1672 days (55.0 months) | | 12 | 0.944 | 139 days (4.6 months) |

The structure allows collusion. Step 4 found no sign of it.

STEP 6

Referee: the objections that changed the result

The skill must raise the strongest objections to its own findings. Here they removed three false alarms and one data error.

Flag

"A cover bidder"

In court-ordered purchases, one firm lost all 8 cochlear-implant items where it met another, always 67–153% dearer.

After checking

Two brands, MED-EL and Cochlear, the world duopoly in hearing implants. A patient fitted with one brand needs compatible parts. Competition between brands, not a cover bid.

Flag

"Bids clustered by design"

Losing bids within 1–3% of the winner, the same trio repeatedly.

After checking

Distributors reselling one single-source drug bought from the same laboratory: a common cost. The pricing power is upstream.

Flag

"Firms that never win"

Bidders with many bids and not a single win.

After checking

They bid in only one or two tenders: their bids were items of the same tender, so they were not independent tries.

Flag

"Two firms taking turns"

A pair of suppliers alternated wins on 22 items; five other "groups" always bid together.

After checking

Each group met in only one to three tenders: the "rounds" were items of the same tender, split by specialty (stents, surgical instruments). One tender is one interaction, not repeated play. The screen now counts tenders, not items.

Flag

"A bidder 58 times cheaper"

One distributor appeared to win an item at a fraction of everyone else's price.

After checking

It quoted per capsule while the others quoted per box. Comparing totals fixed the "probable winner" in 35 items.

  • "Winner" is the lowest bid, not the formal award, which can differ when a bid fails technical requirements.
  • Concentration counts firms by tax ID; corporate groups would make it higher.
  • Items whose bids differ more than fivefold are dropped as incomparable units.
STEP 7

Explain it: the game card

Story

The State buys medicines two ways. When it tenders, few firms show up, and with two bidders the winner keeps a fat cushion. When it buys directly from a patent holder, it haggles with a monopolist, often against a court deadline.

Players

Ministry of Health; laboratories and distributors in tenders; patent holders in direct deals.

Why it is stable

A bidder facing one rival has no reason to cut its price further: the extra chance of winning is not worth the lost margin. A monopolist has no reason to cut its price below what the State's alternative would cost.

Counterintuitive

No cartel is needed for prices to be high. And an alternative the State never uses still sets the price, but only if the seller believes it is real. A court deadline makes it unbelievable.

What to do

Recruit the third bidder; make the outside option credible (reference prices, pooled purchases, technology assessment); publish losing bids as open data so anyone can measure competition.
